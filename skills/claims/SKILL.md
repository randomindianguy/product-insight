# Claims

Three gates. A claim passes all three or gets rewritten.

## Visualize

Can the reader see it? Rigour is invisible. A number that reads 30% and is actually 127% is a picture.

Rewrite an invisible claim into the thing the reader would have seen.

## Falsify

Could the reader check it and catch a lie? An unfalsifiable claim is decoration.

This is also the acceptance mechanism: the easier a human finds it to verify a piece of work, the more they accept it. Verifiability is the persuasive engine, not a nice property of the demo.

## Detachable

Could any competent person say this exact sentence about this exact work? A **detachable** claim is true *of the artifact*, so whoever holds the artifact can say it, and the reader ends up able to champion the work and unable to champion the user.

> *94.5% precision across 128 cases* passes visualize, passes falsify, and is detachable.

This gate fails silently, and it is the one that matters.

## The wall

A detached claim gets attached by finding its **wall**: the exact obstacle the user hit, why it stopped everyone else, and what they saw that the others didn't.

Specificity is the proof of ownership. A principle is borrowable — anyone can write *I lead with the decision* or *I chose precision because false positives kill trust*. An obstacle is not borrowable, because you had to have been standing in front of it. Chrome caps div height at seven million pixels. People can only eval a face-swap on their own face. Case 47 flagged a legitimate renewal and a controller lost trust in one screen. Nobody borrows those sentences, because being able to say them means you were in the room.

Re-voicing from *the system does X* to *I chose X because I believe Y* is necessary and not sufficient. *I chose precision because I believe false positives kill trust* is still a principle, still borrowable. The version with a wall is *I chose precision because case 47 flagged a legitimate renewal and I watched the trust go.* The obstacle is what the **because** was missing.

So attach a claim in two moves:

1. Name the actor and the decision — the user chose X, not *the system did X*.
2. Name the wall behind the decision — the specific obstacle that forced the choice, and why it stopped the people who didn't clear it.

A bullet with no wall is a bullet any competent person could have written, which is the definition of detachable. If you cannot find the wall, the work may genuinely be generic, and the honest move is to lead with different work that has one.

The strongest shape, once earned:

> I predicted this exact failure before I had any data, and the eval confirmed it. The prediction is what I am selling. The score is how I proved it.

That shape requires a written prediction timestamped before the run. Ask to see it. Without one, the honest version is *I flagged this edge as thin during the build*, which still survives a follow-up question.

## Scale is a solvent

A count of how many people got it wrong converts *you out-reasoned me* into *this is a common gotcha*. The same disease as a resume line reading *I did this for twelve companies*: evidence of scale dissolves the particular, personal thing that was persuading. Social proof sells products and detaches claims.

## Done when

Every load-bearing claim is visible, checkable, and names the user as the actor. Each one either has a wall behind it — the specific obstacle only the user could have hit — or has been cut in favour of work that does.
